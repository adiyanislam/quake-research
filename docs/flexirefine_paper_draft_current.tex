\begin{abstract}
% ============================================================
% [CONFIRMED] 36–46% maintenance reduction across 3 seeds.
% [CONFIRMED] All selective configs preserve mean recall >= 0.90.
% [CONFIRMED] Total runtime within 4% of full-refinement baseline.

Dynamic vector search systems must maintain high recall and low query
latency under continuous inserts, deletes, and shifting access patterns.
Partitioned indexes are update-friendly but suffer recall degradation if
maintenance is disabled; enabling full maintenance can dominate total
runtime even under moderate update rates. No existing system exposes a
principled, flexible control surface over the recall--maintenance tradeoff.

We present \system, a control-plane framework that treats local
partition refinement as a tunable resource allocation problem. Instead
of refining all candidate partitions after each split, \system ranks
candidates by a scoring function that jointly estimates workload
importance (via query hit counts) and structural staleness (via
mutation counts since last refinement). This exposes a controllable
tradeoff between maintenance cost, search quality, and total runtime.

We implement a family of refinement policies---including split-threshold
tuning, candidate budgeting, hit-based selection, raw and
density-normalized staleness scoring, and a general multiplicative score
family---and evaluate on a dynamic SIFT1M workload. We report
mean\,$\pm$\,std across three independent workload seeds to account for
K-means non-determinism.

\textbf{Score-based selective refinement reduces maintenance by
36--46\% while preserving mean recall above 0.90 across three
independent workloads, with total runtime within 4\% of the
full-refinement baseline.}
Naive filtering policies (size-only, distance-only, per-split coverage)
reduce maintenance but fail to preserve recall, confirming that a joint
workload-importance--staleness signal is necessary.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Vector search has become a core primitive in retrieval-augmented
generation, recommendation systems, semantic search, and
embedding-based personalization~\cite{todo-rag,todo-annsurvey}.
These applications store large collections of high-dimensional vectors
and answer approximate nearest-neighbor queries under strict latency and
recall requirements. In practice, the underlying data is rarely static.
New embeddings arrive continuously as content is added; stale vectors
are deleted as content is removed; and query distributions shift with
user interests. These dynamics make vector indexing significantly
harder than static approximate nearest-neighbor search.

Existing approaches handle this tension unevenly. Graph-based indexes
such as HNSW~\cite{malkov2018} and DiskANN~\cite{jayaram2019} achieve
strong search quality in read-heavy settings but can be expensive to
update, as graph quality degrades under high-churn workloads.
Partitioned indexes such as Faiss-IVF~\cite{johnson2019} and
ScaNN~\cite{guo2020} are more update-friendly because inserts and
deletes are local operations, but they suffer from partition imbalance
and quality drift if maintenance is neglected. Recent adaptive
systems~\cite{todo-quake,todo-spfresh} show that dynamic maintenance can
substantially improve dynamic partitioned search. However, these systems
expose only limited operating-point choices: maintenance is either fully
enabled or disabled, with few principled options in between.

The deeper limitation is a control problem, not only an algorithmic one.
Different deployments have fundamentally different tradeoff requirements.
A retrieval-augmented generation pipeline may require recall above 0.90
and accept higher maintenance overhead. A high-ingestion streaming
system may want lower maintenance overhead even at a modest recall
penalty. A latency-sensitive recommendation service cares most about
stable per-query latency. A resource-constrained deployment may need an
explicit maintenance budget. A single fixed policy cannot serve all of
these needs. What is needed is a \emph{controllable maintenance system}
that exposes these tradeoffs explicitly, rather than hiding them behind
internal defaults.

This paper presents \system, a control-plane framework for dynamic
partitioned vector index maintenance. \system focuses on
\emph{refinement}, the operation that repairs local partition quality by
running K-means on the neighbors of recently split partitions.
Refinement is important because it reduces overlap and improves vector
assignments, but it is also expensive: in our experiments, disabling
refinement cuts maintenance time by roughly 14$\times$ while dropping
recall by approximately 3 points. This makes refinement a natural point
of intervention---costly enough to matter, important enough that
removing it blindly is unsafe.

\system treats refinement as a resource allocation problem. The key
insight is that refinement is most valuable when applied to partitions
that are both \emph{important to current queries} (estimated by recent
query hit counts) and \emph{structurally stale} (estimated by mutations
since last refinement). Given a maintenance budget, the system ranks
candidate partitions by a score combining these two signals and refines
only the top-ranked subset. This is in contrast to naive approaches that
rank by size, distance, or a fixed per-split quota, each of which we
show fails to preserve the recall target while reducing maintenance.

The score family \system supports is parameterizable. The simplest
effective form uses raw mutation count as the staleness signal:
$S(p) = (h_p + 1) m_p$.
A more principled form normalizes by partition size, treating
\emph{mutations per resident vector} as a proxy for centroid drift rate:
$S(p) = (h_p + 1)(m_p / |p|)$.
The general family $S_{\beta,\gamma}(p) = (h_p+1)^\beta D(p)^\gamma$
subsumes both as special cases and allows exploring the full
importance--staleness tradeoff space.

This paper makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Control-plane framing.}
    We frame dynamic vector index maintenance as a controllable
    tradeoff problem. Different users can select operating points
    based on recall requirements, maintenance budgets, or latency
    stability needs.

  \item \textbf{Full implementation.}
    We implement \system as a modular refinement-control layer on top
    of a dynamic partitioned vector index, exposing all policy knobs
    through configuration files.

  \item \textbf{Score-family formulation.}
    We define a general multiplicative score family
    $S_{\beta,\gamma}(p) = (h_p + 1)^\beta D(p)^\gamma$,
    prove budget-monotonicity and cost-bounding properties, and
    evaluate the family empirically.

  \item \textbf{Multiseed evaluation.}
    We report mean\,$\pm$\,std across three independent workload seeds
    to account for K-means non-determinism, supporting reproducible
    comparison of policy classes rather than individual runs.

  \item \textbf{Negative-result analysis.}
    We show that size-only, distance-only, per-split coverage, and
    hit-only policies each fail to preserve recall while reducing
    maintenance, and explain the mechanism in each case.

  \item \textbf{Baseline comparison infrastructure.}
    We compare against LIRE-style maintenance (size-based policy, no
    K-means refinement) and Faiss-IVF (static IVF, no dynamic
    maintenance), with further baselines (HNSW, ScaNN) planned.

\subsection{Vector Search Indexes}

Vector search indexes organize high-dimensional vectors for approximate
nearest-neighbor retrieval. Graph-based indexes such as
HNSW~\cite{malkov2018}, DiskANN~\cite{jayaram2019}, and
SVS~\cite{aguerrebere2023} navigate proximity graphs at query time,
achieving strong recall-latency tradeoffs in static or read-heavy
settings. Frequent updates can degrade graph quality and require
structural repair, making these indexes expensive to maintain under
high-churn workloads.

Partitioned indexes such as Faiss-IVF~\cite{johnson2019},
ScaNN~\cite{guo2020}, and related IVF variants cluster vectors into
cells and scan a subset of cells per query. These methods are more
amenable to updates because insertions and deletions are local, but
they are sensitive to partition imbalance and centroid drift over time.
\system builds on the partitioned-index design space and adds a
control layer that repairs partition quality selectively after splits.

\subsection{Dynamic and Streaming Vector Search}

Several systems address vector search under continuous updates.
SPFresh~\cite{todo-spfresh} uses size-based partitioning with
periodic centroid recalculation to maintain partition quality
without K-means refinement. LIRE~\cite{todo-lire} similarly employs
reassignment-based maintenance with controlled partition growth.
DeDrift~\cite{todo-dedrift} targets concept drift in embeddings and
rebuilds index structure in response to detected distribution shifts.
FreshDiskANN~\cite{todo-freshdiskann} extends graph-based indexing to
support streaming inserts and deletes with bounded quality loss.
\quake~\cite{todo-quake} uses a cost-model-driven maintenance policy
that decides when to split, merge, or delete partitions based on
estimated scan-latency impact.

\system is complementary to all of these: rather than replacing
the split/merge/delete decision logic, it controls how refinement
effort is allocated after structural changes. LIRE-style maintenance
(size-based, no K-means refinement) serves as a baseline in our evaluation.

\subsection{Adaptive Query Processing and Index Maintenance}

Methods such as APS~\cite{todo-aps}, LAET~\cite{todo-laet}, and
Auncel~\cite{todo-auncel} adapt search-time behavior by controlling
how many partitions are scanned per query, targeting a recall level
rather than a fixed nprobe. SPANN~\cite{todo-spann} combines
graph-based routing with inverted-list scanning for hybrid
latency-recall tradeoffs. These systems focus on \emph{search-time}
adaptivity, while \system focuses on \emph{maintenance-time}
adaptivity. Both are needed for dynamic workloads: search-time
adaptation tunes the current index, while maintenance changes the
future index.

Database adaptive indexing and physical design tuning systems use
cost-model feedback to selectively build and update index structures.
\system shares this philosophy, extending it to the dynamic vector
index setting.

\section{\system System Design}
\label{sec:system}
% ============================================================

\subsection{System Model}

We consider a dynamic partitioned vector index. The index stores vectors
in partitions, each represented by a centroid. Queries search a subset
of partitions and return approximate nearest neighbors. Updates insert
or delete vectors. Maintenance periodically modifies the structure
through splits, deletes, and refinements.

Let $P$ be the set of partitions. Each partition $p \in P$ has:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item size $|p|$,
  \item centroid $c_p$,
  \item recent query-hit count $h_p$,
  \item mutation count $m_p$ (since last refinement),
  \item minimum distance to recent split centroids $d_p$.
\end{itemize}

\system operates during maintenance. When the underlying index decides
that partitions should be split, \system controls the refinement stage
that follows those splits.

\subsection{Architecture Overview}

\system consists of five modules:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Candidate Generator.}
    Given newly split partition centroids, retrieve the $r_f$ nearest
    partition centroids from the parent index per split centroid.
    Deduplicate by keeping the minimum distance per candidate.
  \item \textbf{Metadata Tracker.}
    Maintain per-partition size, query hits, mutation count, and
    distance to split centroids. Update after every operation.
  \item \textbf{Scoring Engine.}
    Compute a refinement score for each candidate using a selectable
    function of hit count and staleness.
  \item \textbf{Budget Allocator.}
    Select a subset of candidates under a global top-$K$ budget,
    per-split budget, threshold, or hybrid policy.
  \item \textbf{Refinement Executor.}
    Apply local K-means refinement to selected candidates.
    Reset mutation counters for refined partitions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

After a split, the candidate generator produces candidate set $C$.
A policy $\pi$ selects a subset subject to budget $B$:
\begin{equation}
  \pi(C, B) \subseteq C, \quad |\pi(C,B)| \leq B
\end{equation}
The ideal policy minimizes:
\begin{equation}
  \pi^* = \arg\min_{\pi(C,B)}\!
    \bigl[\lambda_s T_s(\pi) + \lambda_m T_m(\pi) + \lambda_u T_u(\pi)
          - \lambda_r R(\pi)\bigr]
\end{equation}
This is not solvable online; \system approximates utility through
observable metadata.

\subsection{Candidate Generation}

For each split centroid $s \in S$, retrieve the $r_f$ nearest
partition centroids: $C_s = \mathrm{Nearest}_{r_f}(s,\, P)$.
The global candidate set is $C = \bigcup_{s \in S} C_s$,
with $d_p = \min_{s \in S} \|c_p - c_s\|$.

\textit{Intuition.} The radius $r_f$ is a geometric scope parameter.
It determines where refinement may happen, but distance alone is not
sufficient for final selection.

\subsection{Metadata Tracking}

\textbf{Query hits.} For a sliding window $W$ of recent queries:
\begin{equation}
  h_p = \sum_{q \in W} \mathbf{1}[p \in \mathrm{scanned}(q)]
\end{equation}
Maintained in a circular buffer. Reuses hit data already gathered
for adaptive partition scanning.

\textbf{Mutation count.}
\begin{align}
  m_p &\leftarrow m_p + \Delta_p
    \quad\text{(per insert or delete)} \\
  m_p &\leftarrow 0
    \quad\text{(after refinement)}
\end{align}
Newly split children are seeded with $m_p = \max(1, |p|)$ to enter
scoring as non-trivially stale.

\textbf{Density-normalized staleness.}
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{m}_p = \frac{m_p}{|p|}
  \label{eq:density}
\end{equation}
Estimates the mutation \emph{rate per resident vector}, approximating
centroid displacement more accurately than absolute mutation count.

\subsection{Refinement Control Policies}

\subsubsection{Split-Threshold Tuning}
A partition is split when $\Delta C_{\mathrm{split}} < -\tau_s$.
Higher $\tau_s$ reduces splits. Split-threshold tuning alone reduces
maintenance but cannot recover the recall target (§\ref{sec:eval_split}).

\subsubsection{Refinement Radius and Iterations}
The radius $r_f$ bounds candidate set size; $i_f$ K-means iterations
control repair depth. More iterations reduce overlap more strongly
but show diminishing returns.

\subsubsection{Size-Based and Distance-Based Filtering}
\begin{equation}
  S_{\mathrm{size}}(p) = \mathbf{1}[|p| \geq \tau_{\mathrm{size}}],
  \quad
  p \in \pi \iff d_p \leq \tau_d
\end{equation}
\textit{Weakness.} Neither signal captures workload importance or
staleness. Both are shown to fail in §\ref{sec:eval_negative}.

\subsubsection{Global Top-K Selection}
$\pi(C, K) = \mathrm{TopK}_{p \in C} S(p)$.
Controls maintenance budget directly; requires a strong score function.

\subsubsection{Per-Split Budgeting}
$\pi(C, b) = \bigcup_{s \in S} \mathrm{Top\text{-}b}(C_s)$.
Enforces local coverage per split centroid before the global union.
\textit{Weakness (negative result).} When split centroids are
geometrically close, per-centroid capping breaks global value ranking.
Empirically, no per-split configuration reaches recall 0.90
(§\ref{sec:eval_persplit}).

\subsubsection{Hit-Based Refinement}
$S_{\mathrm{hit}}(p) = h_p$. Prioritizes important partitions but
ignores structural degradation. Insufficient alone.

\subsubsection{Hits-by-Mutations (Raw Staleness)}
\begin{equation}
  S_{\mathrm{hm}}(p) = (h_p + 1)\, m_p
  \label{eq:raw_score}
\end{equation}
Selects partitions that are both important and stale.

\subsubsection{Hits-by-Density (Density-Normalized Staleness)}
\begin{equation}
  S_{\mathrm{density}}(p) = (h_p + 1)\, \frac{m_p}{|p|}
  \label{eq:density_score}
\end{equation}
Empirically, this configuration exhibits lower maintenance variance
than raw staleness scoring at comparable recall levels (§\ref{sec:eval_scores}).

\section{Theoretical Formulation}
\label{sec:theory}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Refinement Utility}

Expected benefit:
$B(p) = \Delta R(p) - \alpha \Delta T_s(p)$.
Net utility:
$U(p) = B(p) - \beta K(p)$, where $K(p) = T_{\mathrm{refine}}(p)$.
Since $B(p)$ is not observable online, \system approximates it.

\subsection{Importance and Staleness}

Importance: $I(p) = f_h(h_p)$ where $f_h$ may be linear, log,
or sigmoid. Staleness: $D(p) = f_m(m_p)$ where $f_m$ may be:
\begin{equation}
  m_p,\quad \frac{m_p}{|p|},\quad \sqrt{m_p},\quad \log(1+m_p)
\end{equation}

\subsection{General Score Family}

\begin{equation}
  S_{\beta,\gamma}(p) = (h_p + \epsilon)^\beta\, D(p)^\gamma
  \label{eq:general}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Properties}

\textbf{Property 1 (Budget monotonicity).}
$B_1 \leq B_2 \Rightarrow
\mathrm{TopK}(C,B_1) \subseteq \mathrm{TopK}(C,B_2)$.
Budget is a monotone control knob.

\textbf{Property 2 (Cost bound).}
$T_{\mathrm{refine}} = O(B \cdot i_f \cdot s_{\max} \cdot d)$.
Worst-case refinement work is bounded by $B$ and $i_f$.

\textbf{Property 3 (Score approximates return).}
If $S(p) = I(p)\,D(p)$ and benefit increases with both signals,
then high-score partitions are exactly those with large joint
importance and staleness. The product suppresses candidates that
are hot-but-stable or stale-but-irrelevant.

\section{System Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Code Structure}

\system modifies four components in a dynamic partitioned vector index:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Maintenance policy} (\texttt{maintenance\_policies.cpp}):
    per-split cap, scoring, budget selection, selective refinement.
  \item \textbf{Partition manager} (\texttt{partition\_manager.cpp}):
    per-partition mutation counter; incremented on add/remove, reset after refinement.
  \item \textbf{Parameter struct} (\texttt{common.h}):
    \texttt{MaintenancePolicyParams} with all new tunable fields.
  \item \textbf{Python bindings} (\texttt{wrap.cpp}):
    exposes all parameters to Python and YAML configuration.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{New Parameters}

All parameters default to values that reproduce existing behavior:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \texttt{refinement\_max\_candidates} (int, $-1$)
  \item \texttt{refinement\_candidates\_per\_split} (int, $-1$)
  \item \texttt{refinement\_size\_threshold} (int, $-1$)
  \item \texttt{refinement\_distance\_threshold} (float, $-1$)
  \item \texttt{refinement\_top\_k\_hits} (int, $-1$)
  \item \texttt{refinement\_top\_k\_score} (int, $-1$)
  \item \texttt{refinement\_normalize\_mutations} (bool, \texttt{false})
  \item \texttt{refinement\_score\_beta} (double, 1.0)
  \item \texttt{refinement\_score\_gamma} (double, 1.0)
\end{itemize}

With all defaults, the score is $(h_p+1) \times m_p$, numerically
identical to the original formula.

\subsection{Selective Refinement Pipeline}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Split partitions produced by the base maintenance policy.
  \item Retrieve $r_f$ nearest neighbors per split centroid.
  \item Apply per-split cap: sort by distance, truncate to cap.
  \item Union into global min-distance map.
  \item Apply optional distance and size filters.
  \item Compute $S_{\beta,\gamma}(p)$ per candidate.
  \item Select top-$K$ candidates.
  \item Run K-means refinement on selected candidates.
  \item Reset mutation counters for refined partitions.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Faiss-IVF caveat.} Faiss-IVF is a static index: its partition
centroids are trained at build time and never updated. Under a 30/20
insert/delete workload, recall degrades progressively as vectors are
inserted into outdated centroid neighborhoods. This is expected
behavior and illustrates the cost of forgoing dynamic maintenance.
Faiss-IVF is included as a no-maintenance reference point.

\textbf{LIRE caveat.} Our LIRE baseline uses the same Quake backend
with size-based partitioning (\texttt{max\_partition\_size}=2000) and
no K-means refinement (\texttt{refinement\_iterations}=0). This
approximates the SPFresh/LIRE design principle of reassignment without
centroid repair. Reviewers should note that our LIRE is a policy
comparison within Quake, not an independent implementation.

\subsection{What Did Not Work}
\label{sec:eval_negative}

\textbf{Size alone is too weak.}
Large-but-stable partitions are prioritized over small-but-stale ones.

\textbf{Distance alone is too geometric.}
Proximity to a split identifies affected neighbors, not queried ones.

\textbf{Budget without ranking refines fewer, not better.}
Global top-$K$ and per-split caps reduce maintenance but rely on a
strong score; without one, they provide no benefit over random selection.

\textbf{Per-split coverage breaks global ranking.}
Forcing $b$ candidates per centroid before the global union prevents
globally high-value selections. Correctness of the bug fix confirmed by
the gap between corrected and buggy per-split numbers.

\textbf{Hits alone are incomplete.}
Frequently queried but structurally stable partitions consume budget
at the expense of stale but moderately queried ones.

\textbf{Super-linear hit amplification ($\beta > 1$) hurts recall.}
The $\beta=1.5$ row is consistently weakest across all $\gamma$ values.

\subsection{Policy Recommendations}

Based on the confirmed results:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Recommended default: Density70-Linear.}
    $S(p) = (h_p+1)(m_p/|p|)$, $K=70$, split threshold 240\,ns,
    2 iterations, radius 50.
    Lowest maintenance variance (CV 12.2\%), competitive recall
    (0.9033 mean), total overhead $+2.9\%$ vs.\ Quake.
  \item \textbf{Lowest-maintenance option: Score50-Raw-Linear.}
    $(h_p+1) m_p$, $K=50$.
    Mean maintenance $-45.9\%$ vs.\ Quake, recall 0.9026.
    Higher variance (CV 24.1\%).
  \item \textbf{Exact $(\beta,\gamma)$ pair is not yet conclusively best}
    at $n=3$ seeds. Claims should be at the policy-class level.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Run Variance and K-Means Non-Determinism}

Refinement quality depends on K-means centroid initialization, which
is stochastic and not controlled by the workload seed. Over a 1000-operation
workload, small differences in early refinement cycles compound into
meaningful behavioral divergence. We observe maintenance cost
coefficients of variation between 12\% and 33\% across three seeds,
and recall standard deviations of $\pm$0.015--0.022. This is why we
report mean\,$\pm$\,std rather than single-run tables for the primary
results, and why claims are at the policy-class level rather than
asserting a specific $(\beta, \gamma)$ pair as optimal.

\subsection{Policy-Class Claims versus Hyperparameter Claims}

The three-seed confirmation establishes that score-based selective
refinement robustly preserves mean recall above 0.90 while reducing
mean maintenance by 32--46\%. The differences between individual
configurations within this class (e.g., Density70-Linear versus
D70-B1.0-G0.5) are within the magnitude of run-to-run variance at
$n=3$ seeds. Firmer per-configuration claims require $n \geq 5$ seeds
or deterministic K-means initialization.

\subsection{Scope of Current Results}

All confirmed results use SIFT1M with a specific 30/20/50 workload mix.
Whether the findings generalize to larger datasets (SIFT10M, MSTuring),
read-heavy or write-heavy mixes, or different dimensionalities is not
yet established. The policy framework is architecture-agnostic, but
empirical validation on multiple datasets is needed for final
conference submission.

\subsection{Baseline Completeness}

The current evaluation compares \system variants against Quake full
refinement and (pending) LIRE and Faiss-IVF. A complete conference
submission requires at minimum one graph-based baseline (HNSW or
DiskANN) and verification that LIRE and Faiss-IVF results are
consistent across seeds. HNSW requires a delete-free workload variant
because the Faiss HNSW implementation does not support vector deletion.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

Dynamic vector indexing systems should expose maintenance as a
controllable resource rather than a fixed internal policy.
\system demonstrates this by treating post-split refinement as a
tunable allocation problem. The key insight, confirmed across three
independent workloads, is that refinement should target partitions
that are both important to queries and structurally stale. Naive
policies that filter by size, distance, or local coverage consistently
fail to preserve the recall target. Score-based selective refinement
reduces maintenance by \textbf{36--46\%} while preserving
\textbf{mean recall above 0.90} with total runtime within
\textbf{4\% of full refinement}.

Among confirmed configurations, density-normalized staleness
($D(p) = m_p/|p|$) provides the most behaviorally stable operating
point (maintenance CV 12.2\%) and is the recommended default.
The optimal exponents $(\beta, \gamma)$ in the general score family
require further confirmation; all tested configurations are competitive.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
% ============================================================

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Complete external baselines.}
    Run LIRE and Faiss-IVF 3-seed comparison; add HNSW (delete-free
    workload) and ScaNN.
  \item \textbf{More seeds ($n=5$).}
    Add seeds 1337 and 2024 to enable firmer per-configuration claims.
  \item \textbf{Deterministic K-means seed control.}
    Expose a random seed in \texttt{refine\_partitions()} to eliminate
    run variance and simplify reproducibility.
  \item \textbf{Larger workloads.}
    Validate on SIFT10M and MSTuring-10M.
  \item \textbf{Gamma fine sweep.}
    With $\beta=1.0$ fixed, sweep $\gamma \in \{0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$
    to locate the optimal staleness exponent.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive budget control.}
    Adjust the top-$K$ budget dynamically based on observed recall drift,
    targeting a user-specified recall threshold rather than a fixed count.
\end{enumerate}
